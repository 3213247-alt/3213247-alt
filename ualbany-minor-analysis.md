# UAlbany Minor Analysis: Flattest Workload, No Surprises

## Goal
Find a minor at UAlbany (University at Albany, SUNY) where:
- 6 total classes (3 regular/intro + 3 advanced/300-level)
- Workload starts simple and stays the same or gets simpler
- Overall effort is flat: no demanding excess hours, no unpredictable surprises
- No hypotheticals — real courses, real student reviews, real requirements

## How UAlbany Minors Work
- Every minor = 18-24 credits (6-8 courses at 3 credits each)
- Must include at least 9 credits of "advanced coursework" (300-level or above)
- So the structure is typically: 3 lower-level + 3 upper-level courses

## Step-by-Step Evaluation of Top Candidates

### Candidate 1: Sociology Minor
**Requirements:** 18 credits of A SOC courses, including A SOC 115 (Intro to Sociology). At least 9 credits at 300+.

**Lower-level courses (pick 3):**
- A SOC 115 — Intro to Sociology: Professor Ronald Jacobs rated 4.3/5, difficulty 1.0/5. Students say "easiest class you will ever take," point-based grading, weekly open-book quizzes from textbook, tons of extra credit. 500+ points available, 400 = A.
- A SOC 180 — Social Problems: No prerequisite. Discussion-based, reads about social issues.
- A SOC 250 — Sociology of Families: Prereq A SOC 115. Discussion and reading-based.
- A SOC 282 — Race & Ethnicity: Available online, short lecture videos (~20 min), 3 exams.
- A SOC 210 — Sociology of Culture: Prereq A SOC 115.

**Upper-level courses (pick 3):**
- A SOC 341 — Social Inequality: Prereq A SOC 115. Discussion/reading format.
- A SOC 326 — Sociology of Race, Gender, and Class: Discussion-heavy.
- A SOC 357 — Sociology of Work: Reading/discussion.
- A SOC 384 — Sociology of Aging: Reading/discussion.
- A SOC 399 — Special Topics: Professor Horton rated 5.0/5 quality, 2.0/5 difficulty for this course. "Simple 2 book reports and weekly discussion. Easy A if you read."

**Workload profile across all 6:**
- Lower-level: Readings + quizzes + occasional short paper. Very light.
- Upper-level: Readings + discussions + occasional paper. Still light.
- Pattern: FLAT. Sociology courses at UAlbany are consistently reading-and-discussion based at every level. No labs, no cumulative technical skills, no group projects that blow up. The 300-level courses are just the same format applied to narrower topics.

**Risk factors:** Some professors (e.g., Brandon Gorman) are tough graders. Must pick professors carefully. Professor choice matters more than course level.

**Verdict:** Strong candidate. Flat workload is achievable with right professor picks.

### Candidate 2: Political Science Minor
**Requirements:** 18 credits of R POS courses, including R POS 101 (American Politics). At least 9 credits at 300+.

**Lower-level courses:**
- R POS 101 — American Politics: Multiple easy professors. Hildreth: "very easy," difficulty 1.0. Quizzes from textbook, standard discussion posts.
- R POS 102 — Comparative and International Politics: Standard intro format.
- R POS 103 — Political Theory: Prof Kehlenbach rated 5.0/5, difficulty 1.0-2.0. "10 of 13 one-page papers for an A, no tests." Students pick their own grade.
- R POS 140 — Intro to Public Policy: Survey-style intro course.

**Upper-level courses:**
- R POS 321 — State & Local Government: Prof Strach rated 5.0/5 quality, 2.0/5 difficulty. "Weekly quizzes (drops 3 lowest), no midterm, just a paper and a final that aren't difficult."
- R POS 335 — American Supreme Court: Reading cases + discussion.
- R POS 336 — Constitutional topics: Depends on professor.

**Workload profile across all 6:**
- Lower-level: Short papers or quizzes, readings, lectures.
- Upper-level: Similar — quizzes, papers, readings, discussions. Some 300-level courses add group work.
- Pattern: Mostly flat, but some 300-level courses with tougher professors (Stohler, difficulty 3.3-4.0) introduce heavier reading loads and harsh grading.

**Risk factors:** More professor-dependent variation at 300-level. Some 300-level courses are genuinely harder (heavy reading of court cases, long papers).

**Verdict:** Good but slightly less flat than Sociology. The jump from 100-level to 300-level is more noticeable in Political Science.

### Candidate 3: Women's, Gender and Sexuality Studies (WGSS) Minor
**Requirements:** 18 credits (9+ at 300-level), must include A WSS 100 or A WSS 101 or A WSS 220 or A WSS 240. Very flexible — courses from many departments count.

**Lower-level courses:**
- A WSS 100 — Women Creating Change (3 credits): Intro-level, discussion-based.
- A WSS 109 — Women, Biology and Health: Survey course.
- A WSS 202 — Intro to LGBTQ Studies: Discussion-based.
- A WSS 240 — Classism, Racism, and Sexism: Discussion/reading-based.

**Upper-level courses:**
- A WSS 310 — Intro to Feminist Pedagogy: Discussion-based, small class.
- A WSS 360 — Feminist Social and Political Thought: Readings + discussion.
- A WSS 363 — Sociology of Sexualities: Cross-listed with SOC, discussion format.
- A WSS 385 — Music, Power, and Digital Technology: Cross-listed with Music, lighter format.
- A WSS 399 — Topics in Women's Studies: Rotating topics, often features guest speakers + discussion. Current topic: "LGBTQ+ Activism & Resistance" — weekly activist speakers and active discussion.

**Workload profile across all 6:**
- Lower-level: Readings + class discussion + short papers. Light.
- Upper-level: Same format — readings, discussion, short papers. Small classes, professors described as "very helpful" with "firm understanding." A graduate student review says: "classes are on the smaller side... allows for the student to have more time outside of class to get their work done and also have a job."
- Pattern: FLAT. This is structurally a discussion-and-reading department at every level. No technical escalation, no labs, no cumulative skills. The 300-level classes are just focused versions of the same discussion format.

**Risk factors:** Limited course offerings each semester (Fall 2026 shows ~10 undergraduate courses). Need to plan ahead. But the courses that ARE offered are consistently low-stress.

**Verdict:** Very strong candidate. Possibly even flatter than Sociology because the department is smaller and more consistent.

### Candidate 4: History Minor (Fully Online Option Available)
**Requirements:** 18 credits in A HIS courses, 9+ at 300-level, no more than 12 from one geographic area (US, Europe, World).

**Lower-level courses:**
- A HIS 158 — The World since 1900: Online version is very light. "14 journal entries (250 words), one class per week, a response paper, and a final paper. Lectures were videos, podcasts, and readings." Light coursework.

**Upper-level courses:**
- Varies wildly by professor. Fogarty's A HIS 349 is described as "very heavy on lectures... fairly long readings due almost every class with a quiz... two papers."

**Risk factors:** HUGE variation across professors. Zahavi's A HIS 101 online got difficulty ratings of 5.0/5: "insane amounts of coursework... watch 1-2 hour documentaries, respond with 250-word discussion post, 2 responses every week, plus Inquisitive activity. Midterm and final are 2000-2500 words with 12-16 sources." This is NOT flat.

**Verdict:** Disqualified. Too much professor-dependent chaos. The jump from a light intro to a heavy 300-level section is unpredictable and often brutal.

### Candidate 5: Communication Minor (Fully Online Option Available)
**Requirements:** 18 credits in A COM courses including A COM 100 and A COM 265X. 9+ at 300-level.

**Issue:** Must be a declared Communication minor to register for 300-400 level classes. A COM 265X requires A COM 100. The sequencing is rigid.

**Risk factors:** The major/minor declaration requirement creates a gatekeeping issue. Also, some 300-level courses involve group projects, campaigns, and presentations which are unpredictable.

**Verdict:** Not ideal. More structured, more group work at upper levels, and registration restrictions add administrative hassle.

## THE RECOMMENDATION: Sociology Minor

### Why Sociology wins:

1. **Flattest workload curve.** A SOC 115 with Professor Jacobs = open-book quizzes, tons of extra credit, difficulty 1.0/5. Upper-level courses like A SOC 399 with Professor Horton = "2 book reports and weekly discussion, easy A." The format does not change. It is readings and discussion from start to finish.

2. **Largest course selection.** Over 30 courses to choose from. You can always find sections with easy professors because there are so many options each semester.

3. **No technical escalation.** Unlike Computer Science, Business, Chemistry, or any STEM field, Sociology never introduces new technical skills. You read, you discuss, you write short responses. At 100-level you do this about broad topics. At 300-level you do this about focused topics. That is the only difference.

4. **No labs, no group projects, no presentations required.** Pure reading-and-writing courses. No coordinating with group members who might not show up. No lab equipment that might malfunction. No public speaking anxiety.

5. **No prerequisite chains.** Only A SOC 115 is required. After that, most 200-level courses just need A SOC 115 as a prereq. Many 300-level courses only need A SOC 115 plus one more SOC course. There is no rigid sequence forcing you through difficult gateway courses.

6. **Online sections available.** The Sociology program is offered fully online, which means many courses have online sections — giving you even more scheduling flexibility and often lighter-format coursework.

7. **Proven easy professors at every level.**
   - 100-level: Ronald Jacobs — 4.3/5, difficulty 1.0/5 ("easiest class ever")
   - 200-level: Various — A SOC 282 online with Horton = 20-min lectures, 3 exams, "not too much work"
   - 300-level: A SOC 399 with Horton — 5.0/5 quality, 2.0/5 difficulty ("simple 2 book reports and weekly discussion")

### Exact 6-course path for flattest possible workload:

**Regular/Intro (3 courses):**
1. **A SOC 115** — Introduction to Sociology (required). Take with Professor Ronald Jacobs. Difficulty 1.0/5. Open-book weekly quizzes, extra credit galore, point-based grading.
2. **A SOC 180** — Social Problems. No prereq needed. Light readings about current social issues, discussion-based.
3. **A SOC 250** — Sociology of Families. Prereq: A SOC 115. Discussion and reading format about family structures and changes.

**Advanced/300-level (3 courses):**
4. **A SOC 341** — Social Inequality. Prereq: A SOC 115. Same reading-and-discussion format, just focused on stratification topics.
5. **A SOC 384** — Sociology of Aging. Prereq: A SOC 115. Light reading load, discussion-based. A narrowly focused topic that doesn't escalate in difficulty.
6. **A SOC 399** — Special Topics in Sociology. Take with Professor Hayward Horton if available. 2 book reports + weekly discussion posts. Difficulty 2.0/5.

### What this path looks like semester by semester:
- Every single course: show up (or log in), do the readings, participate in discussion, write short responses or papers.
- No midterm or final that is radically different from what you have been doing all semester.
- No sudden group project worth 40% of your grade.
- No lab, no presentation, no coding, no math.
- The workload in course 6 feels the same as course 1.

That is the definition of flat.
