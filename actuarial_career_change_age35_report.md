# Actuarial career change at ~35: skills + market welcome (today, and next 7–10 years)

## Scope and sourcing rules (as requested)

- **Included sources**: reports/articles/surveys published by **private companies** (actuarial recruiting firms).
- **Excluded**: government sources (including labor-statistical agencies), universities/public/private educational institutions, and their reports.
- **Geography**: sources are largely **U.S.-market oriented** (because the recruiting/salary surveys are U.S.-focused).

## 1) What changed vs “just pass exams and you’re in”

### Step 1: Identify what private-market gatekeepers currently say “ideal candidates” look like

Ezra Penland (private actuarial recruiting firm) explicitly describes what “the ideal candidate for employers of entry-level candidates” looks like:

- **At least two exams**
- **Internship experience**
- **Strong computer skills including Excel and Access**
- **VBA and SQL and/or SAS are highly desirable**

Source: Ezra Penland, “Entry Level Resources” (`https://www.ezrapenland.com/recruiting-specialties/entry-level-resources/`).

### Step 2: Identify the direction of skill expectations for the next decade (still private-company sourcing)

DW Simpson (private actuarial recruiting firm) frames the “next decade” actuarial skill requirements as a blend of:

- **AI + machine learning** in actuarial work
- Working with **large datasets**
- **Programming** as a differentiator, explicitly naming **Python, R, SQL** as “baseline expectations”

Source: DW Simpson, “The Actuary of Tomorrow: Careers, Skills, and Market Trends” (`https://www.dwsimpson.com/2025/09/29/the-actuary-of-tomorrow-careers-skills-and-market-trends/`).

### Conclusion for this section

Based on what **actuarial recruiting firms** are telling candidates and employers:

- Exams still matter, but **they are not presented as sufficient** for entry-level competitiveness.
- The market signal from recruiters is that **software/data skills are now part of the baseline** (Excel → VBA/SQL/SAS; and for “tomorrow,” Python/R/SQL plus AI/ML literacy).

## 2) Does the market “welcome” newcomers at age ~35 today?

### Step 1: Define what “welcome” means in hiring terms

For a career changer at ~35, “welcome” is not about age as a slogan; it is about whether employers will:

- Interview and hire a candidate into an actuarial role (often **entry-level / near-entry**),
- Provide exam support and progression,
- Accept a profile that may have strong prior professional experience but fewer actuarial years.

### Step 2: Use private recruiting guidance on career change feasibility and trade-offs

DW Simpson explicitly addresses career change feasibility from a recruiter’s point of view: it is possible to move into different work than your prior experience, but may require:

- **Taking a step back financially** (especially when your prior experience implies management expectations but your new target role does not)
- Considering **smaller companies** or **consulting** to broaden responsibilities/projects
- Creating opportunities via **cross-functional work** inside a current employer

Source: DW Simpson, “Charting the Course of Career Change” (`https://www.dwsimpson.com/2021/09/21/charting-the-course-of-career-change/`).

### Step 3: Use private recruiting evidence that “non-traditional” paths exist in practice

DW Simpson also publishes a “non-traditional career path” spotlight, emphasizing skills like communicating technical concepts to non-technical audiences and discussing modern actuarial topics (data/AI bias, predictive analytics).

Source: DW Simpson, “Spotlight on Dorothy Andrews: A Non-Traditional Career Path” (`https://www.dwsimpson.com/2024/11/20/spotlight-on-dorothy-andrews-a-non-traditional-career-path/`).

### Practical conclusion (today)

From these private recruiting sources, the market **does** accept non-traditional entrants—but the “welcome” is conditional:

- **You must look like an employer’s viable hire**, which (per Ezra Penland) includes exams plus tangible computer/data capability.
- **You may need to accept role-level/compensation trade-offs** during the transition (per DW Simpson’s career-change guidance).

## 3) What about the market itself: today’s conditions (without government/labor stats)

DW Simpson’s 2025 recruiting market write-up describes a market shaped by:

- A hiring slowdown continuing into 2024 after late-2023 slowdown
- Employer movement toward **hybrid / in-office** for new hires, with candidates valuing flexibility
- Ongoing emphasis on “new and evolving technology, including AI,” and that candidates aligning capabilities to industry needs will find better prospects

Source: DW Simpson, “2025 Market Trends in Actuarial Recruiting” (`https://www.dwsimpson.com/2025/02/25/2025-market-trends-in-actuarial-recruiting/`).

## 4) Next 7–10 years: will the market still welcome 35+ newcomers?

### Step 1: Anchor the forward view in a private-company “next decade” statement

DW Simpson explicitly frames the actuary’s expanding role and “skills required to thrive in the next decade” as:

- Greater use of **AI/ML**
- More work with **massive datasets**
- **Hybrid actuarial + data science** capability being “most marketable”
- **Programming (Python/R/SQL)** as baseline
- Increased importance of **communication and leadership**

Source: DW Simpson, “The Actuary of Tomorrow: Careers, Skills, and Market Trends” (`https://www.dwsimpson.com/2025/09/29/the-actuary-of-tomorrow-careers-skills-and-market-trends/`).

### Step 2: Translate that into what “welcoming” implies for a 35+ career changer

If employers increasingly value hybrid actuarial + technical skill sets, that generally favors many 35+ career changers **when** they can show:

- Credible technical competence (SQL/Python/R or adjacent analytics tooling),
- Business communication and stakeholder skills (which experienced professionals often already have),
- Exam progress that signals commitment and baseline actuarial knowledge.

At the same time, the same sources imply the barrier is not age—it is **skill + credible fit**, and the transition may still require accepting the “step back” trade-off described in DW Simpson’s career-change guidance.

## Bottom-line answer to your question

- **Yes—career change into actuarial work at ~35 can be viable today**, but it is **not** “just pass exams.” Private recruiting guidance describes employers looking for **exams + concrete computer/data skills** (Excel/Access plus VBA/SQL/SAS; and increasingly Python/R/SQL with AI/ML awareness).
- **The market can welcome 35+ newcomers over the next 7–10 years** if they match the “next decade” skill direction described by private recruiting firms (hybrid actuarial + data/tech + communication). The main practical constraint is that many transitions require a **role/level reset** during the switch, as explicitly noted in recruiter career-change guidance.
