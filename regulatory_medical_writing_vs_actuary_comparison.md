# Regulatory Medical Writing vs. Actuary: 40-Year Career Comparison

## Starting Point
- **Age**: 28
- **Education**: University at Albany, New York - Bachelor's degree (free due to pending asylum)

- **Geographic Context**: United States, with international mobility considerations

## Part 1: Career Entry Requirements

### Regulatory Medical Writer

**Education Requirements:**
- Bachelor's degree minimum (biology, chemistry, pharmacy, or related)
- Master's degree preferred (MS in regulatory affairs, medical writing, or PhD)
- No mandatory licensing exams

**Entry Timeline:**
- Bachelor's degree: 4 years (already completed)
- Master's degree (optional): 1-2 years additional
- Entry-level position: Immediate after bachelor's, or after master's
- **Total time to entry**: 0-2 years after bachelor's

**Certification:**
- Optional certifications (AMWA, RAPS) - not required
- Can work without certification

**Barriers to Entry:**
- Low to moderate
- Writing samples required
- Industry knowledge learned on the job

### Actuary

**Education Requirements:**
- Bachelor's degree minimum (mathematics, statistics, actuarial science, or related)
- Strong mathematics background required

**Entry Timeline:**
- Bachelor's degree: 4 years (already completed)
- First actuarial exam (Exam P): 3-6 months study
- Second actuarial exam (Exam FM): 3-6 months study
- Entry-level position: Requires 2-3 exams passed minimum
- **Total time to entry**: 6-12 months after bachelor's (exam preparation)

**Certification:**
- Mandatory exam series (SOA or CAS)
- Associate level (ASA/ACAS): 5-7 exams, 3-5 years
- Fellowship level (FSA/FCAS): 10 exams total, 5-10 years
- Cannot advance without passing exams

**Barriers to Entry:**
- High (exam-gated career)
- Must pass exams to get hired
- Must continue passing exams to advance

## Part 2: Geographic Mobility Analysis

### Regulatory Medical Writer: International Mobility

**Mobility Factors:**

1. **Regulatory Knowledge Transferability:**
   - FDA (US) regulations differ from EMA (Europe) regulations
   - ICH guidelines provide some international standardization
   - Must learn new regulatory frameworks when moving countries
   - Learning curve: 6-12 months to become productive in new country

2. **Language Requirements:**
   - English is primary language for most regulatory documents
   - Can work in English-speaking countries: US, UK, Ireland, Canada, Australia, New Zealand
   - Non-English countries require local language: Germany (German), France (French), Japan (Japanese)
   - Language barrier: Moderate to high for non-English countries

3. **Professional Recognition:**
   - No universal licensing
   - Experience transfers across countries
   - Portfolio of writing samples demonstrates capability
   - Can work remotely for some companies

4. **Economic Mobility:**
   - Can move to countries with stronger pharmaceutical industries
   - Can work for multinational companies with global operations
   - Can relocate based on economic conditions
   - Remote work options increasing

**Mobility Score: HIGH**
- Can move between countries with 6-12 months adjustment
- English-speaking countries: Easy transition
- Non-English countries: Requires language learning (1-2 years)

### Actuary: Country-Specific Constraints

**Mobility Factors:**

1. **Professional Licensing:**
   - US actuaries: SOA (Society of Actuaries) or CAS (Casualty Actuarial Society)
   - UK actuaries: Institute and Faculty of Actuaries (IFoA)
   - Canadian actuaries: Canadian Institute of Actuaries (CIA)
   - Australian actuaries: Actuaries Institute Australia
   - Each country has separate exam systems
   - Credentials do not automatically transfer

2. **Exam Transferability:**
   - Some exam credits transfer between US/Canada (mutual recognition agreements)
   - UK exams are separate system
   - European countries have separate systems
   - Must retake exams or pass additional exams when moving countries
   - Time cost: 1-3 years to requalify in new country

3. **Regulatory Knowledge:**
   - Insurance regulations are country-specific
   - Tax laws differ by country
   - Accounting standards differ (US GAAP vs. IFRS)
   - Must learn new regulatory environment: 1-2 years

4. **Economic Mobility:**
   - Difficult to move when home country economy weakens
   - Must requalify in new country (1-3 years)
   - Cannot quickly relocate for economic opportunities
   - Stuck in country during economic downturn

**Mobility Score: LOW**
- Requires 1-3 years to requalify in new country
- Exam systems are country-specific
- Cannot quickly relocate for economic opportunities

**Industry Characteristics:**
- Pharmaceutical industry is recession-resistant but not recession-proof
- Drug development continues during recessions (long-term investments)
- Regulatory submissions continue (required for business)
- Budget cuts affect hiring and salaries, not core operations

**Employment Stability:**
- Layoffs occur during severe recessions (2008-2009: 5-10% industry layoffs)
- Hiring freezes common during recessions
- Recovery: 12-24 months after recession ends
- Remote work options provide some protection

**Recession Impact Score: MODERATE**
- More vulnerable than actuaries during severe depressions
- Less vulnerable than most industries
- Can work remotely for international companies

**Historical Evidence:**
- 2007-2009: Pharmaceutical industry layoffs occurred but less severe than other industries
- 2020: Some layoffs, but industry remained relatively stable
- Recovery: Faster than most industries (12-18 months)

### Actuary: Recession Impact

**Industry Characteristics:**
- Insurance industry is highly recession-resistant
- Insurance is mandatory (required by law in many cases)
- Actuaries are essential for regulatory compliance
- Cannot reduce actuarial staff below regulatory minimums

**Employment Stability:**
- Layoffs rare during recessions (2008-2009: <2% industry layoffs)

**Recession Impact Score: HIGH (Very Stable)**

- Insurance is mandatory purchase
- Regulatory requirements protect actuarial jobs

**Historical Evidence:**
- 2007-2009: Actuarial employment remained stable
- 2020: Actuarial employment increased (insurance demand increased)
- No historical evidence of mass actuarial layoffs during recessions

   - Both provide good retirement options
